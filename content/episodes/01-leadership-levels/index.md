## In this episode

- Why most tech organisations have never written down what a team lead, a director or a
  CTO is actually accountable for - and what that costs them.
- The coding continuum: how much should a team lead code, and why both 0% and 100% are
  failure modes.
- Team lead, engineering manager, tech lead: the same job or three different ones? The
  hosts genuinely disagree here, and the disagreement is the useful part.
- Beating the Peter principle with behaviour-based promotion and *acting* roles that come
  with a face-saving way back.
- Why a strong individual contributor ladder - with matching salary bands - is not a
  consolation prize.
- Dunning-Kruger, imposter syndrome, and the "glorious genius" who is brilliant and toxic.

## Deep dive

### The problem: nobody wrote the job down

The episode starts from an observation both hosts keep running into: in most tech
organisations it is simply *not clear* what a team lead is, what a head of engineering
is, or where one role's responsibility ends and the next one begins. Everybody has a
title. Almost nobody has a definition.

That vagueness has two expensive consequences. You cannot coach someone into a role whose
expectations you have never articulated, and you cannot develop an organisation whose
levels only exist as folklore. Growth stalls - not because the people are wrong, but
because nobody can say what "doing this job well" means.

The book that reframed this for both of them is **The Leadership Pipeline** by Charan,
Drotter and Noel. Its core claim is that a career is not one continuous ramp of getting
better at the same thing. It is a series of *passages*, and at each passage the work
itself changes - what you spend time on, what you are measured on, and which skills stop
being relevant. One host describes reading it as a revelation, and now hands it to any CTO
still spending most of their week in the codebase.

### The levels, and what changes at each one

The ladder the episode works with, from the bottom up:

**Individual contributors.** Engineers doing the work. They *do* lead - they lead
themselves, organise their own work, and collaborate inside the team - but their universe
is their own contribution.

**Team lead (first leadership level).** Still close to the code, but now responsible for
one-on-ones, developing people, building a team that works, handling underperformance,
sometimes budget and hiring. Leads individual contributors.

**Director / head of.** Does not lead individual contributors any more. Leads *leaders*.
Coaching team leads is now the job, including coaching them on how much time to spend
leading rather than building.

**VP / CTO / SVP.** Responsible for a functional or business unit - one whole part of the
business, expressed in technical terms.

The point the hosts hammer is not the names, which vary wildly between companies, but that
**the role changes at every step and you have to be aware of exactly how.** A small team of
five needs none of this. The moment you grow past that, you need levels, and the levels
need content.

### How much should a leader code?

This is the episode's most quotable stretch. The framing is a continuum, not a rule.

At 100% coding, a team lead fails - not through lack of skill, but because nobody is doing
the leadership work, and the team quietly misses out on direction, development and
support. At 0%, at the first leadership level, you have usually gone too far the other
way. Where you land in between depends mostly on **team maturity**: a young, new,
enthusiastic team needs coaching, hiring, process and collaboration set up, so the needle
swings hard toward people work. A team that has been together for a decade and hums along
frees the lead up to build more.

Two failure modes bracket the continuum:

1. **The resource-allocation failure.** A manager who treats the team lead as "just another
   resource" and budgets them at 100% delivery capacity - plus people management on top.
   That is the classic route to burnout: the personal problems and team issues do not go
   away just because the sprint board says the lead is fully committed. The hosts'
   practice is not to budget team leads as delivery capacity at all; if they contribute,
   that is upside.
2. **The bottleneck failure.** Higher up the ladder the coding urge is even more dangerous.
   They describe CTOs nominally leading 40, 50, 70 people while writing code 70-80% of the
   time - and, predictably, no team lead below them getting any direction at all. The rule
   of thumb offered: past roughly 10-20 people, you are not writing production code any
   more. Above the team lead level, *do not have tickets assigned to you*, because you
   become a bottleneck for everyone around you.

The analogy that lands: a football coach does not play on the field. The job is simply a
different job.

### Team lead vs engineering manager vs tech lead

Here the two hosts split, and both models get a fair hearing.

**Model A - one accountable lead, paired with product.** In this view "team lead" and
"engineering manager" are two names for the same role: the single leader responsible for
the engineers *and* for the technology being right. Crucially, this lead does not run the
team alone - an engineering team should have **two** leaders, the engineering manager and
the product manager. Product owns *what* and *why*; engineering owns *how*. Add a designer
and you get the trio Marty Cagan describes in **Inspired**. The senior engineers in the
team are consulted heavily - the lead is explicitly *not* expected to be the brightest
person in the room - but the decision sits with one person.

**Model B - split the leadership into a duo.** The other host separates the roles
deliberately: a **team lead** carrying the people and organisational side (roughly 40-50%
of their time in one-on-ones, career development, capacity, staffing) and a **tech lead**
carrying deep technical and architectural leadership. The reasoning: expecting one person
to be simultaneously the best people developer *and* the strongest technologist "puts them
on the spot" unfairly. Better to pair someone strong at people with someone strong at
technology and let them lead together.

Where they agree completely: **accountability must sit with one named person.** If everyone
is responsible, nobody is. Model B keeps accountability with the team lead and splits
responsibility beneath it. Both models also agree that the senior engineer in the room who
knows exactly how to solve the problem should be listened to - the difference is only
whether that person's leadership is formalised into a level.

### Roles as experiments, levels as commitments

A useful distinction the episode draws out: a **role** (scrum master, agile coach, acting
tech lead) is something you hand to someone on top of their level. A **level** is a rung on
the career framework with its own expectations and salary band.

One host uses roles deliberately as *experiments*. Before introducing a principal engineer
level, they wrote a role definition, handed it to a few plausible candidates, ran it for
about a year, and only after seeing the benefit turn it into a real career level. The
warning attached: do not let an experimental role become permanent scaffolding. Long-term
split-brain - "I'm an engineer *and* the scrum master" - forces constant context switching
between two different jobs. Once it works, give it a home of its own.

The counter-position, held by the other host, is to minimise roles and lean on levels,
keeping expected behaviours consistent across the IC and management tracks.

### Promotion without the Peter principle

The Peter principle (Laurence J. Peter, 1969 - and yes, the episode establishes at some
length that it is named after its author, not after an unfortunate employee named Peter)
says people rise to their level of incompetence. The mechanism the hosts identify is
concrete and common: you have twenty engineers, you need a team lead, so you promote the
best engineer. But the skills that made someone the best engineer are precisely *not* the
skills the new job needs. Knowing C++ inside out helps you approximately 0% at leading
people.

Their countermeasures:

**Promote on behaviour, not on performance in the current role.** Look for people already
showing the behaviours the *target* role requires, even while they are still individual
contributors. That is the real signal. Then start a conversation - often the person will
tell you themselves whether they want the management track at all.

**Use acting roles.** Rather than a formal promotion, give the person the responsibility on
an explicitly interim basis, announced as an experiment: we are trying this, we do not know
yet whether it is the final shape. One host runs a structured minimum-one-year *acting team
lead* programme. The point is the **lifeline**: a clear, pre-agreed way back into the old
role. Demotion after a real promotion costs the person their face, and in 25+ years one
host has seen someone voluntarily step back down exactly once. An acting role removes that
trap and lets both sides find out honestly whether it works.

**Own the outcome.** The strongest claim in the episode: if you promote someone and they
struggle, **that is on you, not on them.** You are accountable for setting them up -
training, explicit expectations, mentoring, coaching - and for making the call if it truly
is not working. Promotion is not a reward you hand over and walk away from.

### Career frameworks: you cannot give feedback on an unstated expectation

The career framework (or career ladder) is what makes all of the above operational. It
should say, in writing, what distinguishes a good engineer from a good team lead from a
good director from a good CTO.

The argument for it that stuck hardest: **you can only give feedback on things you talked
about beforehand.** Without a written expectation, "feedback" degrades into complaint -
telling someone six months in that something was part of their responsibility all along.
Set the expectations at the start. This gets harder, and matters more, the higher you go,
because responsibility broadens and the how becomes more open-ended.

The framework also serves hiring: when you have no internal candidate for a director role,
you still need to state precisely what you expect from an external one.

### The IC track is not the consolation prize

Historically, the only way to earn more was to move into management: junior, mid, senior,
then… manager. That is the pipeline that manufactures Peter principle cases at scale,
because a great C++ engineer does not necessarily enjoy - or have any aptitude for -
leading a team.

The fix is a **split ladder**. Shared early rungs (junior, mid, senior, and in bigger
companies senior I/II/III), then a genuine fork: management on one side, staff engineer →
principal engineer → technical fellow on the other. One host makes the equivalence explicit
in compensation: **tech lead and team lead share a salary band; principal engineer and
director share a salary band.** The rungs differ only in what is expected of the person,
not in what they are worth. In their experience, that works well.

### Dunning-Kruger, imposter syndrome, and reading the room

Two psychological effects bracket the promotion conversation.

**Dunning-Kruger** is the failure mode that makes the Peter principle self-sustaining:
someone in over their head who genuinely cannot see it, and believes the work is going
well. The hosts are careful here - this is not stupidity, it is blindness. Which is exactly
why it falls to the manager to see it, have the hard conversation, and either support the
person into competence or make the call.

**Imposter syndrome** (Clance and Imes, 1978) is the mirror image: people doing excellent
work who tell themselves they are the worst engineer alive, and cannot accept praise. It
shows up sharply in interviews - ask someone with imposter syndrome about their best
projects of the last decade and the first answer may well be "nothing, really", which is
almost certainly untrue. The leadership implication is a hiring skill: **calibrate for the
distortion in both directions.** Discount the big mouth who did everything well; probe the
quiet candidate who may in fact have carried the team. The industry's high proportion of
introverts makes this a standing requirement, not an edge case.

### The brilliant, toxic person

The episode's final tangent, and its sharpest disagreement - with a book, not between the
hosts. The setup is Dennis Rodman: so exceptional that the team tolerated him ignoring
every rule everyone else followed. **How Google Works** argues it is a manager's duty to
manage such "glorious geniuses" rather than lose them.

The position taken here is blunt: **no.** Toxic behaviour is contagious, and software
engineering is not professional basketball - there is no publicity value to offset it. What
engineering teams need is a trusting environment where problems can be named openly, and
one person claiming all the glory or blaming others corrodes exactly that. The arithmetic
is simple: hire one, lose three, net minus two.

There is a middle path for the *awkward* rather than the *toxic* - the person who is
brilliant but lands badly in conversation, unintentionally. There, the answer is to find
them the right home in the org: not a stream-aligned team shipping features on a cadence,
but something like a complicated-subsystem team where depth is the job and interaction with
other teams can be proxied through their lead. The important caveat, delivered as the
episode's most emphatic agreement: **build teams around the flow of work, not around
individuals.** And when there is no home - when the behaviour is genuinely toxic and people
are leaving because of it - you make the call, because you are the accountable one.

## Key takeaways

1. **Write the levels down.** Each level needs its own explicit definition of
   responsibilities and expected behaviours. Undefined levels cannot be coached, hired for,
   or given feedback against.
2. **Leadership time is not free.** Do not budget a team lead as full delivery capacity.
   Above the team lead level, stop taking tickets - you become the bottleneck.
3. **Promote for the behaviours of the next role**, not for excellence in the current one.
4. **Use acting roles with an explicit way back**, so trying leadership is not a one-way
   door that costs someone their face if it does not fit.
5. **You are accountable for the people you promote.** Their struggle is your setup
   problem first.
6. **Build a real IC ladder with matching salary bands**, so that management is a choice of
   direction rather than the only route upward.
7. **Team fit beats raw capability.** One toxic high performer costs you more people than
   they replace.
