## In this episode

- Real funnel numbers: 1,300 CVs in, 35 offers out - and what ratio you should actually
  plan against.
- Hiring as a budget item: how many engineer-hours your pipeline consumes, and how to use
  that number in a conversation with management.
- Employer branding, organic and active - plus the underrated reason companies run
  engineering blogs and sponsor conference talks.
- A lean four-stage interview pipeline, why coding challenges stopped being a useful signal,
  and the "pub quiz" tweak.
- Career frameworks and salary bands as hiring instruments, not just internal HR artefacts.
- Six months of probation, used properly - and the mindset flip that makes the final
  conversation a positive one.
- Amazon's Bar Raiser, and the mistake of interviewing for potential when you are hiring a
  senior.

## Deep dive

### Hiring is a numbers game

The episode opens with a real figure from one host's org: roughly **1,300 applicants, 35
contracts**. Whether that is a good ratio depends entirely on where you draw the start and
end lines, but the ratio itself is what matters.

The rule of thumb offered: after screening out obvious mismatches - the C++ CV applying for
a JavaScript role - from **100 genuinely plausible CVs you should expect around 5-10%** to
still be with you after the six-month probation period. Ten percent is a good outcome. It
varies with market, seniority and skill, but you need *your* number.

Why it matters is scaling. If a new initiative needs 30 engineers and you know your
conversion is around 10%, you know how many good CVs must enter the funnel before the first
HR conversation. Then you can watch the pipeline stage by stage: if the CVs are not
arriving, you already know you will miss the hiring goal, and you know it early enough to
change something - passive advertising will not be enough, and you will need active
outreach or to pay someone to bring CVs to you.

### Hiring costs engineering capacity

The second use of the numbers is cost. One host is emphatic: **my department hires for my
department** - engineers run the interviews, nobody else hires on their behalf. That is the
right call for quality, and it is expensive.

The arithmetic is a back-of-the-envelope calculation any leader can do. To hire 10 people
you might run 100 interviews. Each interview is an hour of two engineers' time, plus
preparation and debrief. That is capacity not spent shipping. Now you can say something
concrete to management: *we cannot hire 30 people this quarter, because the interview load
would consume too much of the team - let us stretch it over two or three quarters.* Or you
can decide to shorten the process, or widen the top of the funnel.

The two design constraints that follow: **give candidates real contact with the actual
team** - it is how both sides judge fit - while keeping the total interviewer time as small
as possible. And **be fast**, for the candidate's sake and your own.

### Employer branding: autonomy, mastery, purpose

The framework used to answer "why would anyone join us?" comes from Daniel Pink's *Drive*:
**autonomy, mastery, purpose**.

- **Purpose** - what the company is actually for. And explicitly not "we make a lot of
  money". Every company has a real purpose available to it; finding and articulating it is
  the CTO's job.
- **Mastery** - can someone become the engineer they want to be here? Which technologies,
  which equipment, which problems? A hardware-plus-software company is compelling to a
  certain kind of engineer independently of its mission.
- **Autonomy** - can you grow on your own terms? What support exists to make you
  responsible, accountable, and trusted to decide?

The practical instruction is to get all three into the job description, and into whatever
else represents the company publicly - social posts, videos, the careers page. Job
descriptions are the artefact most companies write carelessly and the one candidates read
most closely.

**Organic vs active.** Organic is posting the description where people find it - LinkedIn,
Glassdoor, campaigns - and waiting. **Active sourcing** is the inverse: talent acquisition
people reaching out directly, the messages in your own inbox.

Active sourcing has prerequisites. Your HR department has to be big enough to do it, which
it usually is not during a hiring surge. Outsourcing it works, at a price, and comes with a
real management burden: the first batches of CVs will be unspecific and often plainly wrong,
and you have to give detailed feedback. One host calls this the **calibration phase** and
notes it applies identically to internal and external sourcers - be very active over the
first dozen CVs. If quality does not improve after calibration, switch sourcer.

The upside of sourced candidates is precision: people who actually match the role, rather
than enthusiastic mismatches consuming interview slots.

**Headhunters** are the next step up, for genuinely hard roles - application security, an
exotic stack, Cobol - and for executive hires. Two reasons they earn their fee: the cost of
an unfilled budgeted role usually exceeds the cost of filling it faster, and executive
searches are often confidential. You may not want competitors or customers knowing you are
hiring for that seat until it is filled. Besides, strong executives are not on the market;
they need a long-term relationship to approach, and headhunters have those relationships
already.

**The long game.** The underrated reason companies run engineering blogs and sponsor
conference talks: **talent attraction.** A candidate reading a good engineering blog can
picture themselves working there. It is slow - no immediate return - but it compounds, it
helps internally too, and adding "by the way, we're hiring" to the bottom of a good post
costs nothing.

### The pipeline

Big tech pipelines - HR hour, screening hour, coding challenge, a full onsite day of seven
interviews - are affordable if you are big tech. For everyone else, spending two engineer-days
per candidate, a hundred times over, means your staff does nothing else.

The lean version, which both hosts converged on independently:

1. **CV screen by HR** - filter obvious mismatches.
2. **HR call, 15-30 minutes** - is this person *real*, and are the non-technical basics a
   fit?
3. **Technical screen, ~30 minutes** - is the technical claim plausible?
4. **Team interview, 1-2 hours** - two members of the team the candidate would actually
   join.
5. **Decision.**

Roughly 15% of candidates reach the team interview stage for senior engineering roles, which
is what keeps the load on the team manageable.

**"Is the person real?"** is not a joke. Most CVs now are polished by AI and look excellent;
the question is whether the human matches the document. And in interviews, candidates using
AI assistance are visible - the one-or-two-second pause before a suspiciously well-formed
answer. Both hosts treat this as the new normal to design around rather than police.

**Coding challenges are out.** Take-home challenges worked two or three years ago, and the
follow-up conversation about the submitted code was genuinely useful - *why this approach,
why test it that way?* In the AI era it is no longer a signal. What replaced it: **live
coding during the interview**, sometimes as pseudo-code in a shared document - the medium
does not matter much - plus questions about past projects.

Two tweaks worth stealing:

- **The pub quiz.** A short multiple-choice technical section run by the HR person during
  the first call, from a prepared sheet. Cheap, fast, and a useful early signal on whether
  the technical interview should happen at all. Calibrated by level: a senior should find it
  easy; a junior getting half right may well be worth continuing with.
- **Sectioned interviews instead of panels.** Rather than three or four interviewers facing
  one candidate, the long team interview opens with everyone, then splits into sections each
  led by **one** person. A second person may sit in as an **observer in training** - because
  the only way to grow more capable interviewers is to have them participate regularly.

**Scorecards and question banks.** HR gets example questions with bullet points describing
what a good answer looks like *and* what the red flags are. The technical and team
interviews have the same: a large bank of questions to pick from, and a simple spreadsheet
scoring matrix - tick the boxes, see where the candidate lands. It makes decisions
comparable across interviewers and across time.

**The CTO's role in all this** depends on the state of the organisation. Joining a company
with no repeatable hiring and a history of bad fits leaving early? Then run the technical
interviews yourself, build the pipeline with HR, and sit in on team interviews to calibrate
scoring. Then write it all down and **aim to replace yourself.** Two goals run in parallel:
hire the right people, and train your people to make the same decisions you would.

### Career frameworks and salary bands as hiring tools

The claim that ties this episode back to [Leadership Levels]({{< relref "/episodes/01-leadership-levels" >}}):
a **career framework is a hiring instrument**. When you open a role - "senior full-stack Java
developer" - the framework should already define what *senior* means, what *full-stack*
means, and how each differs from adjacent levels. The test offered: if your career framework
is not usable in a hiring interview, it is not a good career framework.

And yes, even a startup needs one - because employees want to know how they can progress,
and because you want to pay people fairly. It does not need to be Google's. The simple model
cited comes from Joel Spolsky, on three axes:

1. **Years of relevant experience** - someone with one year is very unlikely to be a senior.
2. **Strategic level** - do they receive work and do it well (professional), drive whole
   projects with a product manager (senior), or coordinate across teams (beyond)?
3. **Skills** - the concrete technical bullet points you define for the role.

**Salary bands** attach to those levels, and they should reflect **market rates**, not
invented numbers. Then when a candidate arrives with an expectation formed by "last salary
plus 20%", you have a defensible answer: this is the band for this level, and it is what the
market pays. In practice most candidates accept, because it is fair - and if your employer
branding and candidate experience are good, you win.

Three refinements:

- **Ask for salary expectations early.** It is a reality check that saves everyone's time -
  and it can be informative: someone applying for a junior role while asking senior money
  might be worth interviewing for the senior opening instead. Sometimes it works the other
  way: they accept the junior offer, because they knew.
- **Think in packages, not numbers.** Fixed and variable components, virtual or real shares.
  It is the hiring manager's responsibility to explain what the compensation is made of, and
  the band definition should cover it.
- **Watch your own signals.** If offers are consistently declined, or too many people leave,
  your band may be wrong. Cross-check against published market-rate reports, and talk to
  other CTOs in your city.

You can also deliberately pay at the top of the band to close faster and make people
reluctant to leave - a legitimate strategic choice.

### Where you hire from

Several structural choices come up together:

- **Language.** Running the company in English while headquartered in Germany widens the
  talent pool enormously - in Berlin, many more people speak English than German,
  particularly specialists.
- **Technology.** You will find more React developers than Flutter developers, and far more
  than Cobol developers. Ease of hiring is a legitimate input into technology decisions.
- **Location.** Nationwide remote hiring is the cheapest expansion - same tax and labour
  law, so no additional load on HR. Europe-wide or worldwide is a bigger step. On-site hiring
  from outside the EU means visas and regulatory timelines.
- **Labour law shapes your process.** US-style hiring can be fast precisely because
  separating is easy. In Germany you get a **six-month probation period** and, after that,
  strong employee protection - which is why both hosts invest heavily up front.

### Use the probation period

The advice here is specific. Six months of probation is not a passive waiting room:

- **Onboard properly.** Laptop ready, credentials ready, a welcoming team, and real work -
  stories, tickets, a project - from the start. Every small thing that does not work makes a
  new joiner wonder what they have joined.
- **Set explicit expectations** for what this role at this level means.
- **Check in at 2 months, 4 months, and 5.5 months** - deliberately before the deadline, not
  at it.
- **Act immediately if it is not right.** Not because the person is bad - it is a mismatch,
  and both sides will be unhappy if it drags on.

Then the mindset flip that is the best moment in the episode. The instinctive question at the
end of probation is *did we find any reason this person does not fit?* Turn it around: **give
me the reasons this person must stay.** A good hire is someone you would fight to keep. That
reframing also changes the conversation the person actually has: not "we found nothing wrong
with you", but "here is what you were great at". Same decision, entirely different message.

### Bar raisers, and hiring for potential

**Amazon's Bar Raiser** gets a fair hearing. The question - *does this candidate raise the
bar for how we operate?* - is a useful thinking tool. The mechanism is the interesting part:
**the bar raiser comes from outside the hiring department**, so a product manager might run
the bar-raiser interview for an engineering hire.

The nuance both hosts land on: not every role needs to clear that bar. For junior hires, the
question is often unanswerable as stated - but juniors bring fresh perspective, which can
itself raise the bar (a junior fluent in AI tooling the company has not adopted, for
instance). The alternative framing offered for juniors is **trajectory**: is this someone who
will move junior → professional → senior over the coming years, who learns, and who may
become a bar raiser *because* of the environment you put them in?

Which leads to the closing warning, and a common failure: **do not interview for potential
when you are hiring for a level.** If you hire a senior, you need someone who *is* senior
now, not someone who could become one. Hiring for potential is a legitimate choice - but you
do not get the capability immediately, and teams that forget this end up disappointed in a
person whose interview process was simply wrong.

A final metric hidden in the numbers: track who was in the loop for hires that failed
probation. Some people are naturally good at evaluating candidates; some are not. That is
visible in your data, and it should shape who interviews.

## Key takeaways

1. **Manage hiring by the numbers.** Know your CV-to-offer ratio, your stage conversions, and
   your post-probation retention. Without them you cannot forecast, budget, or diagnose.
2. **Hiring consumes engineering capacity** - quantify it and use that number in planning
   conversations.
3. **Build employer branding around autonomy, mastery and purpose**, and treat blogs and
   talks as long-horizon recruiting.
4. **Keep the pipeline short and repeatable**: CV screen, brief HR call, brief technical
   screen, one substantial team interview, decide.
5. **Coding challenges no longer signal much** - use live coding and conversations about real
   past work.
6. **Make your career framework usable in interviews**, and attach market-rate salary bands
   to it.
7. **Use the whole probation period** with structured check-ins, and ask "why must this
   person stay?" rather than "is there a reason to let them go?"
8. **Hire the level you need, not the potential** - especially for senior roles.
